# Logo design — the theory and the process this skill works to

Read this before drawing a mark, and again before showing one. Written after two marks were rejected in one session (an unintended silhouette; a cliché carrying a plain letter) and a third request for "5–6 concepts, but study logo design theory first". Sources at the end.

## 1. What a mark is for

A logo **identifies**; it does not explain, and it does not sell. Paul Rand: "a logo derives its meaning from the quality of the thing it symbolizes, not the other way around" — the Mercedes star says nothing about cars, the Lacoste crocodile nothing about shirts; a one-to-one relation between symbol and business "is very often impossible to achieve" and is not required. What *is* required, per Rand's test: **distinctive, visible, adaptable, memorable, universal, durable, timeless — and simple**, because "simplicity is difficult to achieve, yet worth the effort" and complexity "harbors its own self-destruction". And: "likes or dislikes should play no part in the problem of identification" — judge by function.

Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv reduce it to three words in order of importance: **appropriate** (the right feeling for this business), **distinctive** ("unusual in some way — even awkward — to hook the viewer"), and above all **simple** (a simple, distinctive mark survives any later treatment; a complicated one cannot). Haviv starts with pencil and paper and shows marks *in application* (the Armani Exchange mark was rejected on paper and approved on the storefront).

David Airey's five (the working checklist most studios use): **simple, relevant, memorable, timeless, versatile** — relevant to the audience's expectations rather than literal about the product.

Corollaries:

- A mark must be **nameable in a word** ("the swoosh", "the apple", "the stamp"). A mark that needs a paragraph has failed.
- A **wordmark** is the default when the name is short and ownable; a **symbol** needs a reason (a long name, many small surfaces — favicon, app icon, a card — or the product appearing without its name). Most products end as a **combination mark**: symbol + wordmark, each usable alone.
- Fashion is the enemy: gradients, swooshes, mesh, 3-D, sparkles date a mark in three years. Timeless marks are flat, geometric or drawn, in two colours at most.

## 2. The kinds of mark, and when each is right

| Kind | What it is | Right when | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wordmark (logotype) | the name, set distinctively | short unique name; the name *is* the brand | no favicon-sized form; needs a custom feature or it is just a font |
| Lettermark / monogram | one or two letters | long name, or a letter with character | a letter alone is generic — it needs an idea |
| Pictorial (pictograph) | a nameable object | the object is ownable and simple | clip-art reading; industry clichés |
| Abstract (ideograph) | a geometric form | the idea is hard to depict; a global or technical brand | the "amorphous blob"; interchangeable |
| Negative space | a second image in the gap of the first | the hidden element is *single* and *inevitable* | if the primary shape must be distorted to make it, it is forced |
| Emblem / seal | name inside a badge | tradition, certification, institutions | dies at 16 px; text inside a mark is unreadable small |
| Mascot | a character | consumer, friendly, playful | never for a credential, a bank or a hiring product |
| Dynamic / responsive | a system of variants by context or size | large brands with many surfaces | a system without a fixed core has no identity |

## 3. Clichés — the shapes that mean nothing because they mean everything

Do not build a mark on: the checkmark, the shield, the globe, the swoosh, the lightbulb, the "V-man" (person with arms up), the handshake, the gear, the location pin, the padlock, the leaf, the house, the generic "document with a check", the speech bubble. They convey familiarity without brand-specific meaning, and the category is full of them. A cliché may appear as a *component* inside an ownable form; ask whether the mark survives if the cliché is removed. If the answer is "then it is just a P", the cliché was carrying it.

## 4. The tests — run them before anyone else sees the mark

1. **Silhouette.** Fill it black on white at 24 px and look at it *as a shape*. Name what a stranger sees before the idea: circles inside circles, a dot in a round counter, two rounded lobes, a slot on a rounded rectangle (a battery), an arch on a stem (a mushroom), an open book (a library). The eye reads bodies and household objects before letters. An embarrassing or off-brand reading kills the mark; redraw, do not defend.
2. **Blur** to ~3 px. Does the idea survive, or was it in the detail?
3. **16 px.** Anything thinner than 1/16 of the height is gone; the *simple form* must remain (Rand's "visible").
4. **One colour**, black on white and white on black, no tile. A mark that needs its tile is weak.
5. **Other readings**: another letter (P → R, D, B), an arrow the wrong way, a number, a competitor's mark.
6. **Category**: beside the five nearest competitors. *Theirs* or *anyone's*?
7. **Context**: the header, an app icon on a home screen, a tab favicon, the product's own surface, a sign, a shirt. Never present a mark floating alone.

## 5. Construction

- **Sketch first, and many.** Thumbnails an inch square, dozens, before any vector. Quantity produces the unexpected one; the first idea is the cliché. **Black and white first**; colour is added last and must not be what makes the mark work.
- **Grid, then eye.** Build on a unit grid so stroke, counter, spacing and tile radius share ratios and scale together. Then correct optically: round forms **overshoot** flat ones by 1–3%; horizontals slightly thinner than verticals to look equal; the visual centre sits above the geometric centre; white on dark reads heavier than dark on white (irradiation) — thin the reversed version slightly.
- **One stroke weight**, or a deliberate two. Mixed weights read as two objects.
- **Negative space** only as a single hidden element the primary shape yields without distortion; check that unfamiliar viewers find it within a few seconds and that it still registers at thumbnail size.
- **Clear space** ≥ a quarter of the mark's height on all sides; a **minimum size** stated (typically 16 px digital, 8 mm print).
- **The wordmark**: the display face at a heavy weight, tracked tighter than text (−2 to −4%), or one letter custom-drawn to carry the symbol's idea so the pair is one family. Kern it by eye; outline it to paths for the asset files.
- **Colour in the mark**: two colours, three at most; the brand's own hues (`design-theory.md` B2); the mark must also work as one colour. Research (Labrecque & Milne) supports hue → personality only broadly (red/orange excitement, blue competence); choose for the company, not a chart.

## 6. What an identity delivers

- The mark: colour, one-colour dark, one-colour light, one-colour accent; SVG (paths, no text) and PNG at the sizes the platforms want.
- Lockups: horizontal and stacked; on light, on dark, on the brand field. Wordmark outlined.
- Favicon (SVG + 32/16 PNG), app icon (1024 with a safe area; 512/192; Apple touch 180; an Android maskable variant), social image.
- A one-page rule sheet: construction, clear space, minimum size, colours (hex + OKLCH), don'ts (no rotation, gradient, outline, distortion, recolouring), and the parent/product naming rule.
- Where each app takes it from (the source-of-truth path, and every place that must change).

## 7. Presenting concepts

Show **five or six directions that differ in kind** — a lettermark, a pictorial, a negative-space, an abstract, a wordmark-led one — never six variations of one idea. For each: a one-word name; one sentence on what it says about the company; the mark black on white, blurred, at 24 px silhouette, 16 px favicon, 32, 64, app icon, reversed; the header lockup; the browser tab. State which tests each passed, name what was drawn and dropped (and why), give a recommendation with the reason, and stop. **Nothing is applied until the user picks.** Expect to iterate on the chosen direction, not to re-present all six.

## 8. Before adopting

Search for confusingly similar marks in the category and the app stores before a name and mark are committed to; this is a note, not legal advice — a trademark search is a lawyer's job.
