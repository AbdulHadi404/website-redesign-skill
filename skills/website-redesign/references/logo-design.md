# Logo design — the theory and the process this skill works to

Read this before drawing a mark, and again before showing one. Written after two marks were rejected in one session (one for an unintended silhouette, one for being weak): both were drawn straight into code from a single idea, without the discipline below. Sources at the end.

## 1. What a mark is for

A logo **identifies**; it does not explain. Paul Rand: the only mandate is that it be *distinctive, memorable and clear* — and its effectiveness comes from distinctiveness, visibility, adaptability, memorability, universality and timelessness, with simplicity as the means. Chermayeff & Geismar / Sagi Haviv reduce the test to three words, in this order of importance: **appropriate** (right feeling for the business), **distinctive** (memorable, "unusual in some way, even awkward, in order to hook the viewer"), and above all **simple** (a simple, distinctive mark can survive any later treatment; a complicated one cannot). Choosing a logo "is not about what one likes or dislikes: it's about what works."

Corollaries:

- The mark does not have to *depict* the product. It has to be nameable in a word ("the swoosh", "the apple", "the arrow") and feel right for the company. A mark that needs a paragraph has failed.
- A **wordmark** is the default when the name is short and ownable; a **symbol** needs a reason (a long name, many surfaces, a favicon and app icon, a product that appears without its name). Most products end up as a **combination mark**: symbol + wordmark, each usable alone.
- Fashion is the enemy: gradients, swooshes, mesh, 3-D, "AI" sparkles date a mark in three years. Timeless marks are flat, geometric or drawn, and two colours at most.

## 2. The kinds of mark, and when each is right

| Kind | What it is | Right when | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wordmark | the name, set distinctively | short, unique name; the name *is* the brand | no favicon-sized form; needs a custom feature or it is just a font |
| Lettermark | one or two letters | long name, or a letter with character | a letter alone is generic — needs an idea |
| Pictorial | a nameable object | the object is ownable and simple (apple, target, bird) | clip-art reading; industry clichés |
| Abstract | a geometric form | the idea is hard to depict; a global or technical brand | "amorphous blob"; interchangeable |
| Negative space | one image formed by the gap in another | the second reading feels inevitable, not forced | if the primary shape must be distorted to make it, it is not working |
| Emblem / seal | name inside a badge | tradition, certification, institutions | dies at 16px; text inside a mark is unreadable small |
| Mascot | a character | consumer, friendly, playful | never for a credential or a bank |

## 3. Clichés — the shapes that mean nothing because they mean everything

Do not build a mark on: the checkmark, the shield, the globe, the swoosh, the lightbulb, the "V-man" (abstract person with arms up), the handshake, the gear, the location pin, the padlock, the leaf, the house, a generic "document with a check". They convey familiarity without *brand-specific* meaning, and the category is full of them. A cliché can appear as a *component* inside an ownable form (a checkmark as the foot of a letter is still a checkmark); ask whether the mark survives if the cliché is removed. If the answer is "then it is just a P", the cliché was carrying it.

## 4. The silhouette test, and the other tests

Run these on every candidate, in this order, before anyone else sees it:

1. **Silhouette.** Fill the mark black on white at 24px and look at it as a shape. Name what a stranger sees before they see the idea. Circles inside circles, a dot in a round counter, two rounded lobes, a slot on a rounded rectangle (battery), an arch with a stem (a mushroom) — the eye reads bodies and household objects before letters. If any reading is embarrassing or off-brand, the mark is dead; redraw, do not defend.
2. **Blur.** Blur it to ~3px. Does the idea survive, or was it in the detail?
3. **16px.** Render at 16×16. Is it still recognisably itself? Anything thinner than 1/16 of the height is gone here — design so the *simple form remains* when the detail goes.
4. **One colour.** Black on white and white on black, with no tile. Many marks only work as "white glyph on the brand tile" — a mark that needs its tile is a weak mark.
5. **Rotate the reading.** Does it read as another letter (a P that reads as R or D), an arrow the wrong way, a number?
6. **Category.** Put it beside the five nearest competitors' marks. Is it *theirs* or *anyone's*?
7. **Context.** Show it in the header, as an app icon on a phone home screen, as a tab favicon, on the product's own surface (a card, a screen). Haviv's Armani Exchange story: a mark rejected on paper was approved the moment it was shown on the storefront and the billboard. Never present a mark floating alone.

## 5. Construction

- **Sketch first, and many.** Thumbnails, an inch square, dozens of them, before any vector. Quantity produces the unexpected one; the first idea is usually the cliché. Work in **black and white**; colour is added last and must not be what makes the mark work.
- **A grid, then the eye.** Build on a unit grid so stroke, counter, spacing and tile radius share ratios and scale together. Then correct optically: round forms **overshoot** the flat ones by 1–3% or they look small; horizontal strokes are drawn slightly thinner than verticals to *look* equal; the visual centre sits a little above the geometric centre; a white mark on dark reads heavier than the same mark dark on white (irradiation) — thin it slightly for the reversed version.
- **One stroke weight**, or a deliberate two. Mixed weights read as two objects.
- **Negative space** only when it is a *single* hidden element that the primary shape produces without distortion; test that unfamiliar viewers find it within a few seconds and that it still registers at thumbnail size.
- **Clear space** of at least a quarter of the mark's height on all sides; the lockup's wordmark sits outside it.
- **The wordmark**: the display face at a heavy weight, tracked tighter than text (−2 to −4%), or a custom drawing of one letter that carries the symbol's idea so the two are one family.

## 6. Presenting concepts

Show **five or six directions that differ in kind** (a lettermark, a pictorial, a negative-space, an abstract, a wordmark-led one), not six variations of one idea. For each: a one-word name, one sentence on what it says about the company, the mark at 16 / 32 / 64 / 160, in one colour both ways, on the brand tile, and in context (header, app icon, favicon, the product surface). State your recommendation and why, and say which tests each concept passed. The user picks; nothing is applied until they do.
